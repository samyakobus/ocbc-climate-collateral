# Climate Risk Sensitivity of OCBC: Research Briefing

## 1. Bottom line

OCBC is moderately sensitive to climate risk today and materially sensitive on a 2030-2050 horizon, with the transmission running through property collateral rather than borrower default. Every supervisory stress test that has separated books finds transition risk hits corporate/wholesale lending and physical risk hits residential mortgages, where losses concentrate in a small share of locations (HKMA: 25x mortgage ECL uplift; BoE: half of mortgage losses from 10% of postcodes) [20][22]. OCBC's housing book is S$72.6bn (~21% of S$341bn gross loans, end-2025) [24], Singapore has ~30% of land below 5m and SLR risk is already capitalised into HDB prices [9][10], yet no Singapore-specific retail-mortgage climate-loss estimate exists because MAS's exercises excluded retail and its first physical-risk analysis only began in 2026 [5][7]. The binding regulatory date is September 2027, when MAS expects banks to value high-physical-risk collateral on "sufficiently granular physical hazard estimates" [2]. OCBC itself admits its geolocated mortgage work is new, non-Singapore-specific and blind to adaptation [25].

## 2. Regulatory pressure in Singapore

MAS regulates through principles, not haircut rules, but the expectations have become concrete:

- **2020 ENRM Guidelines (effective June 2022)** name impaired collateral as the primary credit channel, expect portfolio metrics that "consider the impact of environmental risk on its collateral valuations", scenario analysis informed by "statistical models to determine the frequency of flooding events", and annual TCFD-aligned disclosure [1].
- **Transition Planning Guidelines (finalised 5 March 2026, effective September 2027)**: for high-physical-risk customers or sectors, banks should determine "the current value and realisability of collateral based on sufficiently granular physical hazard estimates", may factor in "jurisdiction-level adaptation projects", and should use scenario results to trigger "adjustments of collateral valuations" and feed ICAAP. Banks must build a climate data strategy and document proxy/vendor data limitations; SMEs and non-corporate collateral are in scope on a risk-proportionate basis [2][3]. Note: the opportunity angle dates these to 2025; the primary MAS documents say March 2026.
- MAS declined a hard rule on climate-adjusted collateral but stated physical shocks "can emerge as short-term shocks, manifesting within capital planning horizons", and pointed banks to CCRS V3 downscaled data (SSP126/245/585) [3].
- **Supervisory practice**: MAS's May 2022 paper shows banks already capping LTVs or requiring insurance in at-risk areas, with one bank sizing ICAAP capital for mortgage devaluation; geospatial collateral data was flagged as the key gap [4]. MAS ran NGFS-based scenarios in IWST 2022 [5], a transition study in 2023 [6], and in 2025/26 its first top-down acute-physical-risk analysis with IMF assistance (results unpublished) [7].
- **Disclosure**: as an STI constituent OCBC makes full ISSB-based disclosures from FY2025 and Scope 3 from FY2026; OCBC currently applies IFRS S2.30 transition relief on quantitative physical-risk metrics [8][25].

## 3. Physical risk to mortgage collateral

Singapore-specific evidence:

- After the Aug 2019 National Day Rally SLR map, HDB resale prices in exposed zones fell relative to nearby controls, but far less where adaptation was announced; private/freehold property showed little response. **Angles disagree on magnitude**: the published JPubE figures are reported as 7.2% vs 0.6% (physical-collateral angle) or 5.8% vs 2.3% (opportunity angle); an earlier working paper reported 2.5%. Verify against the journal PDF before quoting [9]. A smaller NTU study finds a 2.54% flood-prone discount [17].
- Hazard: ~30% of land is <5m above mean sea level; the Third National Climate Change Study projects up to 1.15m SLR by 2100 and ~2m by 2150 (up to 5m with surge and tide) [10]. Peer-reviewed V3 projections give median 0.42m (low emissions) to 0.75m (high) by 2100 [11]. Bloomberg/CBRE put ~S$70bn of prime real estate at high flood risk [17]. PUB's flood-prone-area list is public on data.gov.sg [17].
- Scale: property is ~56% of household net worth; housing loans ~S$236-247bn outstanding [17][7].

International benchmarks for discount size: SLR-exposed US homes trade ~7% lower, up to 22% for the most exposed [12]; US coastal prices exceed fundamentals by ~13%, with flood overvaluation of US$121-237bn [13]. The main transmission channel is insurance withdrawal: UK modelling projects 430,000 "climate mortgage prisoners" by 2050 and >20% value declines in the highest-risk areas [14]; Australia projects 1 in 25 homes effectively uninsurable by 2030 [15]. Euro-area banks now charge 4-37bp physical-risk premia on mortgages [16]. Singapore insurance data is a gap: contents cover excludes flood by default and no premium or withdrawal data was found [17].

## 4. What stress tests say about loss sensitivity

- **Retail mortgages are the physical-risk book.** HKMA pilot (Dec 2021): 32% of HK property collateral in vulnerable areas; residential mortgage 1-year ECL rose 25x (HK$0.7bn to HK$17.3bn), insurance recoveries excluded [22]. BoE CBES: impacts "highest for banks' wholesale and mortgage exposures", mortgage losses largest under the physical (NAA) scenario, ~45% of impairments from 10% of postcode areas [20]. ECB 2022: mortgages "more vulnerable" than corporate real-estate-secured loans outside the highest-risk areas; Dutch mortgage LGD rose from 4% to 7% average and 18% in high-risk locations [18][19]. APRA: mortgage loss rates up to 3x historic by 2050, 20% of postcodes carry ~75% of losses, and banks responded by cutting maximum LVRs [21].
- **Corporate is the transition-risk book.** CBES high-carbon sectors: 35% cumulative impairment, twice the corporate aggregate [20]. MAS 2023: abrupt transition costs 14.2% vs 6.0% of net profit [6].
- **Singapore**: IWST 2022 covered only CPRS and sovereign exposures; retail was excluded. Even so, a 1-in-200-year ASEAN-5 flood cost banks ~15% of a year's net profit, physical risk outweighed transition risk, and banks conceded their methods likely underestimate losses [5]. NGFS Phase V raised chronic physical damage to ~15% of GDP by 2050 under current policies, but the underlying Kotz et al. function was retracted [23].
- Every exercise flags collateral location data and insurance assumptions as the binding constraints [18][20][21][22].

## 5. OCBC's own position and admitted gaps

- **Trajectory**: 2023, postal-code/flood-map matching of collateral for ICAAP and the HKMA stress test [27]; 2024, granular geolocation capability still "in the process" of development [26]; 2025, in-house hazard models (cyclone, coastal/fluvial/pluvial flood) that convert projected collateral loss into an LGD uplift, with 2050 RCP8.5 flood maps for Singapore, KL and Hong Kong [25].
- **Retail coverage**: OCBC "began monitoring physical risk exposures in our retail mortgage... portfolios" only in 2025, and names Indonesia and Hong Kong (not Singapore) for the hazard-map overlay [25]. (The 2023 work was a stress-test exercise, not portfolio monitoring, so the two disclosures are consistent.)
- **Stated limitations** (SR2025 p.51): "incomplete or imprecise location information"; assessments "do not account for existing adaptation measures, government-led climate resilience initiatives"; limited to pledged collateral; heat stress not modelled; "use cases beyond climate scenario analyses" unexplored; no percentage of exposed collateral disclosed [25].
- Net-zero targets cover 42% of corporate/commercial loans and exclude mortgages; ESG Risk 2.0 underwriting enhancements are corporate-only; no climate-attributed defaults observed in Singapore [25][28].
- **Peers**: UOB completed a consultancy-built flood/typhoon model in 2025 covering retail mortgages at 30x30m resolution, reporting "minimal impact" for the ~70% of assets in Singapore [29]. DBS has a geolocation "climate risk data mart" but deems climate immaterial and values mortgage collateral by appraisal and HPI [30].

## 6. Where the business upside is

- **Pricing**: the ECB finds a 4-37bp premium per SD of physical risk, concentrated in banks judged "adequate" by supervisors [16].
- **Portfolio steering**: Bain/Jupiter model 10-15% collateral erosion and a 7-10% mortgage profitability hit by 2050 unmanaged, versus 15-20pp NOI uplift by 2030 with LTV caps, insurance and low-risk-asset discounts (modelled, European) [31].
- **Capital and provisions**: granular data lets a bank defend ICAAP and ECL with evidence rather than blanket overlays; the ECB applies climate haircuts to pledged collateral from H2 2026 and BIS WP 1274 gives an IRB-compatible method [34]. RBA/XDI: high-LVR loans rise 2.75pp on average by 2050, unpriced by markets [15].
- **Template**: Climate X's Tier 1 UK bank case (0.5m properties) quantified provisions, reset postcode appetite and moved to real-time underwriting APIs [33].
- **Demand**: OCBC Eco-Care loans hit S$3.5bn in two years and ~a quarter of new home loans by count in 2022 [28]; DBS says 40% of 2025 launches qualify for green loans [35]. Caution: UK evidence finds green mortgage discounts (~9bp) are product differentiation with no credit-risk relationship [32]. No Singapore bank has published a S$ ROI, and vendor pricing is unpublished.

## 7. Implications for the hackathon product

Supported by evidence:
- **Property-level hazard-to-collateral-value engine** for Singapore mortgages, outputting LTV/LGD adjustments: matches MAS 2027 expectation [2], OCBC's LGD-uplift methodology [25], and the location-concentration finding in every stress test.
- **Adaptation-aware valuation** (Long Island, coastal protection, PUB drainage): the Singapore price evidence shows adaptation status roughly eliminates the discount [9]; MAS explicitly allows it [2]; OCBC explicitly lacks it [25].
- **Scenario layers** using CCRS V3 / Ng et al. SLR distributions and RCP8.5-2050 flood maps [3][11].
- **Data-quality and proxy documentation** (address geocoding confidence, vendor assumptions): a named MAS obligation [2] and OCBC's first-listed limitation [25].
- **ICAAP/IFRS S2 output** (share of collateral in hazard zones): fills OCBC's S2.30 transition-relief gap [8][25].

Not supported, or should be framed carefully:
- Claiming lower PD for green or resilient homes [32].
- Heat stress and storm effects on Singapore values: unquantified [17].
- Insurance-withdrawal dynamics for Singapore: no data [17].
- A hard S$ loss figure for OCBC's Singapore book: none exists; present any number as an illustrative scenario, and pick one Agarwal et al. figure only after verifying the journal text [9].
- Positioning as "new capability": OCBC already has hazard models; the pitch is integration into origination/revaluation, Singapore retail coverage and adaptation-aware data quality.
