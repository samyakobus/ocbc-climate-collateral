# Group-level view: Great Eastern and Bank of Singapore

## 1. Great Eastern

Great Eastern Holdings (GEH) is a life insurer with a small general-insurance tail. In FY2024 the Non-life segment produced S$443.0m of insurance revenue and S$24.0m of after-tax profit, against S$6,461.2m and S$703.4m for Life, on total assets of S$113.9bn [1]. Direct catastrophe underwriting is therefore roughly 6% of insurance revenue, and it is cushioned by catastrophe reinsurance placed with reinsurers rated at least S&P A- [1].

The larger climate channel is the investment book: debt securities S$13.3bn, collective investment schemes S$43.5bn, loans S$21.6bn and investment properties ~S$1.4bn at end-2024 [1]. GEH reports PCAF-based financed emissions and holds 2030 targets (listed-equity footprint -40%, corporate bonds -45% vs 2020), with progress of -34.2% and -50.2% respectively at Dec-2024 [2]. On physical risk, however, its Sustainability Report 2024 says it has only "begun to model" flood and cyclone vulnerability of the GI book across three NGFS scenarios, and that "current model results contain a substantial degree of uncertainty" [2]. No quantified physical-risk loss is disclosed.

The regional evidence base is real. Malaysia's December 2021 floods caused RM6.1bn of economic loss with RM2.2bn (36%) insured per BNM's FSR 2H2021 [3]; Malaysian Re put insured loss at RM1.5-2.0bn and warned of "substantially" higher reinsurance pricing and demands for more granular exposure data [4]. Flood cover remains an optional rider bought on ~33% of fire policies and ~14% of motor policies in 1H2023 [5], and DOSM data show only 5.5% of 2025 flood victims held insurance [6]. Regulators are tightening: MAS's Dec-2020 ERM Guidelines for insurers require scenario analysis and TCFD-style disclosure [7], the March 2026 Transition Planning addendum (effective September 2027) requires physical-risk assessment inside transition planning [8], and BNM's 2024 Climate Risk Stress Test was mandatory for GELM, GETB and GEGM with a flood-centred physical component [9].

## 2. Bank of Singapore

Bank of Singapore (BOS) is OCBC's private bank, with AUM of US$142.6bn at end-June 2025 and its US$145bn 2025 target surpassed by end-September 2025 [10][11]. Group wealth-management income was a record S$5.60bn (38% of total income) in FY2025 [12]. Its booking centres — Singapore, Hong Kong, Dubai DIFC, London, Luxembourg — are all low-lying coastal wealth hubs, so property collateral and clients' own real-estate holdings are geographically correlated on physical risk [13].

BOS already links sustainability to collateral: since July 2021 it adds 5 percentage points to the advance ratio on Lombard loans secured by MSCI ESG AAA/AA-rated funds [14], and it runs quarterly Climate Value-at-Risk on discretionary mandates [15]. There is no public evidence of any physical-climate adjustment to real-estate collateral or Lombard haircuts. At group level OCBC states it "completed physical risk model for flood and tropical typhoon" in 2025 but publishes no exposure figures and does not mention BOS [16].

The hubs are exposed. About 30% of Singapore's land lies below 5m above mean sea level, with projected sea-level rise of up to 1.15m by 2100 [17]; Bloomberg/CBRE estimated S$70bn of prime real estate at high flood risk (Oct 2023) [18]; Sentosa Development Corporation names Sentosa Cove as at risk from flooding and saltwater intrusion [19]. Hong Kong's 2021 HKMA pilot stress test found roughly a third of property lending secured on coastal or low-lying property, with potential devaluation above 50% and residential-mortgage ECL rising from HK$0.7bn to HK$17.3bn under a high-emissions 2050 scenario [20]. Yet markets are not pricing this: Sentosa Cove's 2023-25 price decline (landed psf -13.1%) is attributed to ABSD and supply, not flooding [21], and Dubai villa values rose after the April 2024 floods even as UAE property insurance rates climbed up to 15% in Q4 2024 [22]. Climate-adjusted collateral valuation at BOS is thus a regulatory-readiness and risk-management differentiator, framed for clients as "climate resilience of your assets" advisory.

## 3. Cross-arm synergies for the product

- **Shared hazard layer.** One geocoded flood/typhoon/sea-level layer can serve OCBC mortgages, GEH's GI underwriting and investment-property book, and BOS's property collateral. GEH's stated modelling gap [2] and BNM's demand for granular exposure data [4][9] make the insurer a natural co-owner of the layer rather than a downstream consumer.
- **Insurance-mortgage feedback loop.** US evidence shows insurer withdrawal and repricing feeding into mortgage delinquency (1.2% to 3.5% after Hurricane Irma) and house prices [23][24]. In Singapore and Malaysia the observed response is repricing, higher deductibles and tighter terms rather than withdrawal, and the dominant collateral problem is under-insurance [3][5]. A tool that flags uninsured or under-insured collateral lets GEH sell flood riders through its existing OCBC bancassurance channel [1] while the bank holds better-protected security — the same data both prices the rider and adjusts the LTV.
- **Wealth-client property.** BOS clients concentrate in the exact enclaves the hazard layer scores (Sentosa Cove, HK coastal districts, Dubai villas, Thames-side London). Extending the collateral tool from mortgage LTVs to Lombard and real-estate haircuts, plus a client-facing resilience report, satisfies MAS's 2027 expectations on physical-risk customer engagement without "indiscriminate withdrawal of credit" [8].

## Research gaps noted by agents
- great-eastern: GEH does not disclose GI claims by peril, catastrophe reinsurance retention/limits, or the Dec-2021 flood claims paid by GEGM; no company-specific flood loss figure was found.
- great-eastern: GEH Sustainability Report 2025 (ISSB-aligned) could not be located; figures above are from SR2024 (Dec-2024 data).
- great-eastern: Aggregate published results of BNM's 2024 CRST and quantitative results of MAS's 2022 IWST flood overlay (exact % rise in property claims) are not available in accessible public text; the MAS deck chart was not machine-readable.
- great-eastern: MAS website returned 'service unavailable' for the original 2020 insurer guidelines PDF and FSR 2023 PDF; requirements were confirmed via law-firm summaries (Allen & Gledhill, Linklaters, CMS) rather than the primary text.
- great-eastern: No Singapore- or Malaysia-specific empirical study linking insurance repricing/withdrawal to mortgage default or house prices was found; the feedback-loop evidence is US-based (Florida).
- great-eastern: GEH's investment-portfolio physical-risk exposure (e.g. share of real estate/loans in flood-prone locations) and sovereign-bond financed emissions are not disclosed.
- great-eastern: Precise breakdown of GEH's S$17.2bn gross premiums into life vs general (GWP basis) is not given in the AR beyond segment insurance revenue and subsidiary GWP for GEG Singapore and GEGM Malaysia.
- bank-of-singapore: No public figure for BOS's loan book, its split between Lombard and real-estate-backed lending, or the geographic distribution of property collateral; OCBC reports wealth AUM and group loans (S$341bn) but not BOS lending separately.
- bank-of-singapore: OCBC/BOS do not publish quantified physical-risk exposure (share of mortgages or collateral in flood-prone areas, scenario loss estimates); only a qualitative statement that a flood/typhoon physical-risk model was completed in 2025.
- bank-of-singapore: The primary MAS guideline pages and HKMA stress-test documents could not be fetched (site unavailable / empty response); MAS content is from CMS Law and Asian Banker summaries and the HKMA 32% / >50% / 25x figures rest on secondary summaries (Eye on ESG, Lexology, SCMP).
- bank-of-singapore: No peer benchmark found showing any global private bank (UBS, Julius Baer, HSBC PB) publicly applying climate-adjusted haircuts to Lombard or real-estate collateral, so the 'differentiator' claim is by absence of evidence rather than confirmed.
- bank-of-singapore: No dated 2025-26 survey isolates Singapore HNW demand for climate risk assessment of personal property; sustainable-investing demand figures are from Lombard Odier's earlier APAC studies (2021-2023 vintage).
- bank-of-singapore: Sentosa Cove flood-risk price effect has not been studied quantitatively; the widely-cited '20-30% erosion by 2050' figure is from an unsourced marketing blog and should not be used in the pitch.
- bank-of-singapore: Great Eastern's role (insurability of HNW property, premiums as a leading indicator) was outside this angle and not researched here.
